## Overview

In October 2024, I spent a month at **SICK's** office in Canton, Massachusetts, working alongside American colleagues on cloud infrastructure projects. As a DevOps Engineer, my role centers on making developers' lives easier by ensuring system reliability, automating workflows, and optimizing infrastructure. This trip reminded me that DevOps isn't just about infrastructure and pipelines. It's about people, processes, and knowledge transfer.

## A Typical Day in Canton

My mornings started around 8:30 with infrastructure health checks: reviewing container orchestration status, monitoring system metrics, and planning the day's work. The rest of the day involved meetings, collaboration sessions, and hands-on work with tools like **Kubernetes** for container orchestration, **Terraform** for infrastructure as code, and **Ansible** for configuration management.

The goal was always the same: ensure systems are scalable, reliable, and optimized for customer needs. Whether designing cloud infrastructure or debugging deployment pipelines, the focus remained on automation, monitoring, and continuous improvement.

## Knowledge Transfer with a Retiring Engineer

One of the most valuable aspects of the trip was working closely with a colleague preparing to retire. His decades of experience held institutional knowledge that couldn't simply be documented in Confluence pages or runbooks. Through daily collaboration (architecture discussions, infrastructure reviews, and troubleshooting sessions), I absorbed insights about system design patterns, common pitfalls, and the reasoning behind past technical decisions.

## Beyond the Keyboard

The trip wasn't all terminals and YAML files. Colleagues welcomed me with classic American hospitality: a dinner featuring steak, corn on the cob, Brussels sprouts, and apple pie. They also served as tour guides through New England's stunning fall foliage, from New Hampshire's Artist's Bluff to Cape Cod's LeCount Hollow Beach.

At LeCount Hollow Beach, I asked if the water was good for swimming. My colleague's response? "Sure, if you're interested in seeing some sharks." I stayed on the shore. The fall foliage was beautiful enough from a safe distance.

These moments mattered. Sharing meals, hiking trails, and casual conversations built trust that made technical collaboration smoother. You learn how colleagues think, communicate, and approach problems. That context strengthens teamwork long after you return home.

## The Value of Being There

While remote work offers flexibility and efficiency, certain situations benefit from being in the same room. Whiteboard sessions for complex architecture discussions, spontaneous troubleshooting, and real-time feedback loops can accelerate understanding when time-sensitive decisions are needed.

This trip wasn't about proving remote work doesn't work. It works exceptionally well for most tasks. But for knowledge transfer from a retiring engineer and building relationships with a team you'll collaborate with remotely for years? Being there made all the difference.
